The model scored 93.61%. Then we tested a new person.

Building a wearable activity-recognition system taught me that a good validation score is only the beginning. This case study follows the difference between training-distribution results, unseen people, and the monitoring work that came next.

01 / The question

How can we tell whether a model is reliable when the next person's movements differ from the people it learned from?

The input came from a Garmin Venu 3 smartwatch: accelerometer and gyroscope measurements on the wrist. I worked with a 1D-CNN–BiLSTM model that estimates eleven activities, including hand tapping, hair pulling and nail biting. It operates on four-second windows, with each new window starting two seconds after the previous one. Because an activity label is not always available for new data, the pipeline also records confidence and monitors unusual patterns.

02 / What the evidence showed

The numbers changed the story.

The cross-validation results looked very promising. But evaluation that left out entire people revealed a much harder problem: user-to-user differences. I did not treat that as a reason to hide the result. It shaped the monitoring logic, the review decisions, and the rule that model replacement should require a human decision.

Limits and context. The labeled cohort is small, sensor placement and user habits vary, and monitoring thresholds are engineering choices rather than clinical guarantees. Takeaway

A model can be accurate on familiar data and still be unready for unfamiliar people. The system around the model matters.
